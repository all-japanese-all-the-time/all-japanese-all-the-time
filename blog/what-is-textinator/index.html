What Is Textinator?

Move over, letters. Bye-bye, fax machines. Step aside, telegrams. Take a knee, email. Put a sock in it, phone calls. It’s 2016, and we are well into the era of the smartphone. And that means that text messaging is the premier, the prime, the primary mode of communication in our world today.

Don’t believe us? Go count the number and volume of text messages you sent this week. Then do the same for email. Case closed.

Yeah, they’re short, but we send a lot of them. And so in terms of both message count and message volume, they’re how we communicate now. According to some statistics [citation needed] at least a quarter of a million text messages get sent through the ether every second.

And literally — literally — everybody is doing it. You see attractive couples at restaurants and cafes, staring down at their shiny rectangles, tapping and swiping and stroking away. You see toddlers in strollers stroking their digital squares. Even your mother keeps texting you, like a needy girlfriend, always with the dumb questions.

Damn that mother of yours.

ANYWAY!

You didn’t need us to tell you that texting is popular. So we haven’t actually helped you yet. Allow us to change that starting now.

As you probably already know (but have forgotten), beginning at least in the 1990s and continuing into the early 2000s, Japan had both the highest cellphone penetration rate in the world (mind out of the gutter — that just means that everybody* had at least one if not two) and the most advanced terminals and carrier services in the world. * 1

Beginning with NTT DoCoMo’s “iMode”, mobile Internet and cellphone cameras have been a thing in Japan since back when most people in the world (if they were alive) were just grateful to have broadband at home — if that — let alone on their cellphones.

Also, because Japan’s cellphone carriers were so competitive and wary of one another, they could never agree on a communication standard like SMS (which is what text messaging is called in much of the “Old World” — Africa, the Middle East and Europe, and anywhere else that uses the GSM system so…pretty much everywhere except China, Japan and the United States), so they just ended up using actual email — SMTP and POP over TCP/IP which had the benefit of being proven, stable, independent, third-party, open standards which put nobody in danger of being screwed. As a result, text messaging in Japanese is just called “(cellphone e)mail” (メール or 携帯メール).

It’s called “email” but it’s nothing like regular email. It differs completely in tone, content, interface and general nature. Text messaging in Japanese (as in every other language) is a distinct mode of communication with its own idiosyncrasies, rules, etiquette and assumptions.

No matter what the language though, all text messaging shares these traits: it has to be short, it has to be fast (unlike snailmail (post) or email, you can’t make people wait a week for a response so you can craft that perfect message) and it has to make sense. That sounds easy. But it isn’t. Because, you see, the shorter your message, the more confusion one error can produce. Add to that the fact that everyone — especially females — expects, no, demands, a quick response and you’re headed up excrement creek without a paddle.

But don’t worry. Because thankfully…

We’ve gone and made you a paddle.

Many people think that texting is the death of prose. Texting, they wail, is the end of writing — and by extension, of civilization itself. Nothing could be further from the truth. Texting is nothing less than the rebirth of concise, epigrammatic, aphoristic, haiku (俳句)-like writing on a massive scale.

Sending texts in Japanese requires you to raise your game to a new level. A higher level. It requires you to be able to dynamically consume and produce short, precise Japanese sentences in writing (duh) with correct grammar. Fast. On the fly. That means that, smartphone notwithstanding, the Japanese needs to be in your head already: there’s no time to look it up.

The world has gotten faster, and you need to get fast to keep up with it.

And that’s why we made you:

Textinator: How to Read and Write Real Japanese Text Messages So You Won’t Be Up Excrement Creek Without a Paddle.

Because nobody else was gonna do it. Have you seen what Japanese textbooks are like? First of all, most of them assume that gaijin are idiots who will never learn to read. And the few that don’t have a condescending, illiteracy-promoting agenda haven’t even gotten ’round to acknowledging the existence of the Internet, let alone email and texting. So, if you’re depending on the JSL (Japanese as a Second Language) education establishment to help you out of this or any other hole and bring you into the bright, shiny, soft-edged rectangular light of the early 21st century, then you’re screwed. You might as well join a cult right now — the cult people will lie to you, too, but at least they’ll give you a sense of purpose and warm fuzzies inside.

Where were we? Oh yeah. Textinator!

The Textinator isn’t for everyone. You need to fulfill the following three requirements to qualify for the privilege of owning it:

  1. Know hiragana and katakana down pat. Like, completely. No ifs, ands or buts. No romaji bandits allowed.
  2. Know at least 100 kanji. No kanji virgins allowed.
  3. Own a smartphone or tablet. Because, otherwise, like, WTF, right? Like, why would you even…
